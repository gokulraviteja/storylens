# Story, Script, and Retention Standard

## Objective

Every line must sound spoken, create a visible idea, and give the viewer a reason to continue. Accuracy is necessary but does not compensate for weak storytelling.

## The line-utility rule

Every line must perform at least one useful job:

- Advance the situation
- Create or deepen a question
- Increase the stakes
- Reveal surprising information
- Explain something necessary
- Produce a clear visual idea
- Deliver an earned joke
- Reframe an earlier belief
- Pay off an open question

Important lines should usually perform two jobs. Delete or combine lines that only repeat context.

## Opening structure

For strange-evidence Shorts, test this opening pattern:

```text
Did you know...? → name the strange object → reveal the impossible-sounding evidence
```

Complete all three steps within roughly six seconds and use a different visual beat for each. “Did you know?” is not the hook by itself; the specific fact after it must be surprising without exaggeration.

The first 30 seconds should normally:

1. Place the viewer inside a concrete moment.
2. Introduce an immediate problem, discomfort, or contradiction.
3. Confirm the promise made by the title and thumbnail.
4. Reveal that the obvious explanation is incomplete.
5. Promise the question the video will answer.

Do not begin with a logo, channel introduction, broad historical background, or disclaimer. Integrate necessary reconstruction language after attention has been earned without misleading the viewer.

## Narrative engine

Build the video as a chain rather than an article:

```text
situation → problem → attempt → complication → evidence → partial answer → larger question → payoff
```

- Maintain one main question.
- Introduce a smaller unanswered question approximately every 20–40 seconds when natural.
- Close open questions; do not create mystery without payoff.
- Move unusually strong evidence or entertainment earlier instead of saving all value for the ending.

## Human-sounding language

- Write with contractions and ordinary verbs.
- Vary sentence length deliberately.
- Allow fragments, asides, and pauses where a speaker would naturally use them.
- Prefer concrete objects and consequences over abstractions.
- Let humor emerge from the situation or modern contrast.
- Avoid portraying historical people as ignorant merely to create a joke.

Flag repeated formulas such as “But here's the thing,” “What happened next,” “This changed everything,” symmetrical three-part lists, vague dramatic claims, and overly polished documentary phrasing.

## Read-through gate

Before voice production:

1. Read the script aloud at the intended pace.
2. Listen once without looking at the text.
3. Mark the first attention drift.
4. Mark lines that cannot produce a meaningful visual.
5. Rewrite those passages.
6. Test the opening with people who have not seen the outline.

Use the detailed [narration evaluation](narration-evaluation.md) for scoring.

## Working performance targets

These are experimental baselines, not universal laws:

- General narration: approximately 155–175 words per minute
- Faster delivery: comic lists or controlled escalation
- Slower delivery: important evidence, emotional turns, and payoffs
- Brief pause before a reveal; room after the strongest joke or conclusion

Never speed-stretch a whole narration merely to meet duration. Rewrite or direct the performance.

## Visual-beat expectations

Frequent change must be meaningful and easy to process:

- First 15 seconds: a new visual idea approximately every 1.5–3 seconds
- 15–60 seconds: approximately every 2–4 seconds
- Remainder: approximately every 3–6 seconds
- Every 15–25 seconds: consider a pattern change such as a close-up, diagram, map, comedic cutaway, evidence card, or audio reset

A visual change can be a new approved image, alternate generated pose, crop, object state, image-based diagram step, expression, layer animation, or motivated camera move. Code-drawn visuals do not qualify. Random cuts and constant motion do not count.

## Prototype gate

No full video proceeds to final visual production until a representative 45–60 second prototype—or the complete cut of a 25–35 second Short—contains final-quality:

- Script
- Voice performance
- Visual cadence
- Illustration treatment
- Limited animation
- Music and sound effects
- Caption treatment

The creator must approve the prototype. Technical completion alone is not a pass.
